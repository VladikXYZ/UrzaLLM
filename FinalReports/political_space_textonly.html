Urza in the political space · which general political topics does he address? · textonly

Everything so far measured Urza against himself. Here the reference is independent of him: a map of politics in general (22 policy areas, 85 subtopics, 168 concrete questions, 73 concepts of political philosophy). Urza's 2,721 chunks (767 article, 976 book, 978 video) are projected into that space to see which parts of it he attends to, how deeply, and where his blind spots are.

What was done

  1. The political space (Questions/political_space.tsv, editable): the policy areas of the Comparative Agendas Project, an academic classification of what governments deal with, adapted to Czech politics and extended by “Morality and personal freedom”; under each area its subtopics, under each subtopic 1–3 concrete questions as Czech voters meet them; and a separate layer of concepts (state, legitimacy, social contract, ideologies, rights, justice, economics, ethics). 326 items in total, drafted for this report.
  2. Embedding: the same model as Urza's texts (bge-m3), computed on this machine; a test showed identical vectors to the GPU run (cosine 1.0000). Topics and concepts are embedded as “name: description”, questions as written.
  3. Depth (per topic, question, concept): the similarity of Urza's single best-matching passage, exactly as a search query would rank them. From the checks below, passages above 0.55 are typically on topic and above 0.60 squarely on it; these thresholds are a reading aid, not a hard boundary. Also counted: how many different texts reach 0.55.
  4. Volume (per area): every chunk is assigned to the nearest subtopic, whose centre is the average of the subtopic and its questions. As in the earlier reports, each source's average vector is subtracted first, so the style of book prose or video descriptions doesn't decide the outcome. Announcements are left out.
  5. Map: the political items alone are laid out with UMAP, after subtracting the average vector of each item type separately (topics, questions, concepts). Each type is worded in its own way (a question versus a “name: description” entry), and without this the map sorts items by type rather than by subject, the same format effect as between Urza's sources. The map is then fixed, and Urza's chunks are added into it without refitting, in one of three ways (switch above the map): among nearest items, at a similarity-weighted average of the positions of the five items each chunk is most similar to; UMAP transform, UMAP's own method for placing new points into a fitted map, with each chunk's source average subtracted (as everywhere in this report: this removes both what all of Urza's texts share and what is typical of articles, books or videos); or UMAP transform, one Urza average, the same but with a single average of all his chunks subtracted, which removes his overall “Urza-ness” and keeps the differences between the three sources.

How to read it: nearby items are about related things. A policy area is often spread over several parts of the map (questions about the state touch many subjects), so each area label sits on its most central item. Because each item type was centred separately, items no longer group by how they are worded: 40% of an item's 10 nearest neighbours on the map are of the same type (chance 38%; 92% without the centring), and 27% of a topic's or question's neighbours belong to the same policy area (chance 5%; 12% without the centring). The depth and volume numbers do not depend on the map. Dark blue items are ones Urza addresses squarely, pale ones only weakly; the cloud of small dots shows where his texts gather. Hover an item for its best passages.

Nearest items vs UMAP transform: in all three placements, the political items stay exactly where they are; only Urza's chunks are placed differently. Among nearest items spreads them over 851 of 1,600 grid cells (the 5% densest cells hold 27% of the chunks) and never puts a chunk outside the area of the political items. UMAP transform reaches more cells (968) but also piles more chunks onto a few spots (33% in the densest 5%), and it lets some fall away from the items (11% land outside the items' area). A chunk moves by a median of 0.12 map widths between the two. Neither keeps a chunk next to the items it is most similar to: the item closest to a chunk on the map is one of its five most similar items for only 27% (nearest items) and 27% (transform) of chunks, because a 2D map cannot keep 326 items and thousands of passages in their true neighbourhoods. Read both maps for where his texts gather overall; for which items they are about, use the depth numbers and the hover panel, which do not depend on the placement.

One Urza average vs one average per source: the third placement removes only what all of Urza's texts share. It reaches 971 cells (densest 5%: 33%), 11% land outside the items' area, and the nearest item on the map is one of the five most similar for 26% of chunks. It keeps the differences between sources, but they barely show: among a chunk's 10 nearest chunks on the map, 39% come from the same source (articles, books or videos), against 36% with the per-source averages removed, 37% for “among nearest items”, and 34% if sources were mixed at random. So once Urza's overall average is removed, the remaining differences between articles, books and videos hardly change where his texts land in the political space; a chunk moves by a median of only 0.05 map widths between this placement and the second one (against 0.12 between the first two).

Caveats

  • The list defines the result. It was drafted for this report and is one reasonable map of politics, not the only one. It deliberately includes a few classic questions from the anarcho-capitalist debate (“Who would build the roads if there were no state?”, “Is taxation theft, or a legitimate fee for public services?”), which Urza naturally covers well. Edit Questions/political_space.tsv and rerun both scripts to change it.
  • Similarity is not agreement. A high match means Urza discusses the question, not which answer he gives.
  • Videos are represented by their descriptions, so subjects he talks about at length in videos but doesn't name in the description are under-counted.
  • Volume depends partly on how finely an area is divided: an area with more subtopics offers more centres to be closest to (the state and public administration has 6, migration 2). Depth doesn't have this problem, since it is an average over the area's items.
  • Thresholds are a reading aid. The depth values are continuous; 0.55 and 0.60 were chosen from the spot checks.

Conclusions

  • Urza covers a large part of the political space: 93% of the questions have at least a relevant passage and 54% a strongly matching one; his texts reach 85 of the 85 subtopics.
  • His attention is uneven, in a recognisable way: most of it goes to “Government and public administration” (11%), “Law and justice” (10%), “Education” (9%), “Business and finance” (9%); the deepest answers are in “Education” (0.64), “Business and finance” (0.62), “Social policy” (0.62), “Transportation” (0.62).
  • Blind spots lie in areas of conventional policy-making; the lowest depth is in “Agriculture” (0.57), “Environment” (0.57), “European Union and international relations” (0.55), “Energy” (0.53). Questions such as “Should a new nuclear power plant be built?”, “Should genetically modified crops be allowed?”, “Is a conflict of interest of a businessman prime minister unacceptable?”, “Should the Czech Republic remain in NATO?”, “Should neighbors' consent be all an owner needs to build on their own land?”, “Should foreign policy promote human rights even at the cost of trade losses?” get no passage that addresses them directly. An anarchist framework tends to answer them only through general arguments about the state.
  • Next step for UrzaLLM: the blind-spot questions are where a debating assistant would have to reason from Urza's general principles rather than quote him, and where it should say so.
